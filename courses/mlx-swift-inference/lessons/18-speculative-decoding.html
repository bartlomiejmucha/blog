18. Speculative decoding

Guess several tokens cheaply, then check them all with one pass of the real model. Learn the acceptance rule that keeps output exactly as good as normal decoding, and the cost model that says when it actually speeds things up.

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Explain draft-then-verify and why verification is nearly free when decode is bandwidth-bound
  • Apply the greedy acceptance rule
  • Apply rejection sampling so sampled output keeps the target model's distribution
  • Predict the speed-up from acceptance rate and relative costs

The idea

Lesson 2's table again: a single-sequence decode step barely uses the GPU's arithmetic. Running the model on 2 or 3 tokens instead of 1 reads the same weights and, ideally, costs about the same. Prefill already exploits this. Can decode?

The obstacle is that decode is sequential: token n+1 depends on token n. Speculative decoding gets around it by guessing:

  1. A cheap drafter proposes the next token, or the next few.
  2. The real model, the target, runs once on the last committed token followed by the drafts. Because a transformer produces logits at every position, this one pass gives the target's prediction after each draft as well.
  3. Compare: keep the drafts the target agrees with, plus one token from the target itself at the first disagreement or after the last draft.

Every step emits at least one token, the same one normal decoding would have produced, and sometimes more. The drafter can be a small separate model, a lookup in the prompt, or, in Qwen3.5's case, a tiny head built into the model itself (next lesson).

Greedy acceptance

With greedy decoding the rule is simple. Suppose the last committed token is t and the drafter proposes d. The target runs on [t, d] and returns two sets of logits:

  • at position 0: what should follow t. Call its argmax a₀.
  • at position 1: what should follow d. Call its argmax a₁.

If a₀ = d, the draft was right: emit d and also a₁, two tokens for one pass. If not, emit a₀, which is what plain greedy decoding would have chosen, and discard everything computed for d, including its entries in the caches.

The output is identical to plain greedy decoding, in principle token for token. In practice the two-token pass uses a different kernel shape than the one-token pass, so a near-tie can occasionally flip (Lesson 8). Lesson 19 shows exactly such a case.

Sampling without changing the distribution

With temperature above zero, "is the draft right?" has no yes/no answer. Let q be the drafter's distribution and p the target's, both after the same temperature and truncation (that is why the sampler in Lesson 10 exposes logProbabilities). The draft d was drawn from q. Then:

  1. Accept d with probability min(1, p(d) / q(d)).
  2. If rejected, draw a replacement from the leftover distribution max(0, p − q), normalized.

Why this is exact: the chance of ending with token x is the chance it was drafted and accepted, q(x) · min(1, p(x)/q(x)) = min(q(x), p(x)), plus the chance of a rejection times the leftover's probability for x. The total rejection probability is 1 − Σ min(p, q) = Σ max(0, p − q), which is exactly the normalizer of the leftover, so the second term is max(0, p(x) − q(x)). Adding them, min(q, p) + max(0, p − q) = p(x). The output is distributed exactly as if the target had sampled alone. Where the drafter overestimates a token, some of its drafts are rejected; where it underestimates one, the leftover makes up the difference.

When it is faster

Let α be the acceptance rate with one draft per step. Each step then emits 1 + α tokens on average. Measure costs in units of one normal decode step:

  • c_verify: the target pass on 2 tokens. Ideally about 1, if the step really is bandwidth-bound.
  • c_draft: producing the draft.

speed-up = (1 + α) / (c_verify + c_draft)

With α = 0.9, c_verify = 1.0 and c_draft = 0.15, the speed-up is 1.9 / 1.15 ≈ 1.65. But if the 2-token pass costs 1.4 normal steps, it drops to 1.9 / 1.55 ≈ 1.23, and at 2.2 the "speed-up" is a slowdown. c_verify is the number that decides everything, and Lesson 15 already measured that it depends on the matmul kernel and the weight precision. With k drafts per step, the expected tokens per step become (1 − α^(k+1)) / (1 − α), but c_verify and c_draft grow with k too. Drafters that are only trained to look one token ahead, like Qwen3.5's, use k = 1.

Lesson in one breath

A cheap drafter proposes tokens; the target model scores the last committed token plus the drafts in one pass. Greedy: accept a draft if it equals the target's choice. Sampling: accept draft d with probability min(1, p(d)/q(d)), otherwise draw from the normalized leftover max(0, p − q); this yields exactly the target distribution. With acceptance rate α and one draft per step, each step emits 1 + α tokens on average, and the speed-up is (1 + α) divided by the cost of a verify step plus a draft, in units of a normal step.
